## Commerce Clause (Article I § 8)

Congress's power to regulate interstate commerce, including its three categories (channels, instrumentalities, and activities substantially affecting interstate commerce) and the dormant Commerce Clause limit on state regulation.

Landmark cases:

| Case | Citation | Year | Court | Holding |
| --- | --- | --- | --- | --- |
| Wickard v. Filburn | 317 U.S. 111 | 1942 | Supreme Court of the United States | Congress may regulate purely intrastate activity if, viewed in the aggregate, it has a substantial economic effect on interstate commerce; upheld federal wheat-quota limits as applied to a farmer's home consumption. |
| United States v. Lopez | 514 U.S. 549 | 1995 | Supreme Court of the United States | Struck down the Gun-Free School Zones Act as exceeding Congress's commerce power; reaffirmed that the regulated activity must substantially affect interstate commerce and clarified the three-category framework. |
| Gonzales v. Raich | 545 U.S. 1 | 2005 | Supreme Court of the United States | Congress may regulate purely intrastate, noncommercial cultivation of marijuana for personal medical use because, in the aggregate, such activity substantially affects the interstate market in controlled substances. |
| National Federation of Independent Business v. Sebelius | 567 U.S. 519 | 2012 | Supreme Court of the United States | The ACA's individual mandate exceeded Congress's commerce power because the Commerce Clause does not authorize compelling individuals to engage in commerce, but was sustained as a valid exercise of the taxing power. |

## Content-based vs content-neutral speech restrictions

The First Amendment test for distinguishing content-based from content-neutral regulations of speech.

Landmark cases:

| Case | Citation | Year | Court | Holding |
| --- | --- | --- | --- | --- |
| Reed v. Town of Gilbert | 576 U.S. 155 | 2015 | Supreme Court of the United States | A law is content-based on its face if it draws distinctions based on the message a speaker conveys; such laws are subject to strict scrutiny regardless of motive. |
| Ward v. Rock Against Racism | 491 U.S. 781 | 1989 | Supreme Court of the United States | Content-neutral time, place, and manner restrictions are constitutional if narrowly tailored to serve a significant government interest and leave open ample alternative channels. |
| R.A.V. v. City of St. Paul | 505 U.S. 377 | 1992 | Supreme Court of the United States | Even within a category of unprotected speech, the government may not engage in content or viewpoint discrimination; struck down a hate-speech ordinance for selectively prohibiting fighting words based on subject matter. |

Canonical statutes: U.S. Const. amend. I.

## Counterman v. Colorado - mens rea for 'true threats' prosecutions

The First Amendment requires the State to prove a 'true threats' defendant was at least reckless - i.e. consciously disregarded a substantial risk that the communication would be viewed as a threat.

Landmark cases:

| Case | Citation | Year | Court | Holding |
| --- | --- | --- | --- | --- |
| Counterman v. Colorado | 600 U.S. 66 | 2023 | Supreme Court of the United States | The First Amendment requires the State to prove a 'true threats' defendant subjectively understood (at least recklessly) the threatening nature of his statements; a purely objective standard chills protected speech. |
| Virginia v. Black | 538 U.S. 343 | 2003 | Supreme Court of the United States | True threats are statements where the speaker means to communicate a serious expression of intent to commit an act of unlawful violence; states may proscribe such threats consistent with the First Amendment. |
| Watts v. United States | 394 U.S. 705 | 1969 | Supreme Court of the United States | A statute punishing threats against the President must be interpreted to require a 'true threat,' not political hyperbole. |

## Defamation and the actual-malice standard

The constitutional fault standard for defamation of public officials and public figures: knowledge of falsity or reckless disregard for the truth (actual malice).

Landmark cases:

| Case | Citation | Year | Court | Holding |
| --- | --- | --- | --- | --- |
| New York Times Co. v. Sullivan | 376 U.S. 254 | 1964 | Supreme Court of the United States | A public official may not recover for a defamatory falsehood relating to official conduct unless he proves the statement was made with 'actual malice' - with knowledge that it was false or with reckless disregard of whether it was false or not. |
| Gertz v. Robert Welch, Inc. | 418 U.S. 323 | 1974 | Supreme Court of the United States | A private figure need only show fault (at least negligence), not actual malice, to recover actual damages for defamation; states may not impose liability without fault. |

## Equal Protection in race-conscious admissions (post-SFFA)

After Students for Fair Admissions v. Harvard (2023), race-based admissions programs face strict scrutiny that the prior Grutter framework no longer survives; most race-conscious admissions are unconstitutional.

Landmark cases:

| Case | Citation | Year | Court | Holding |
| --- | --- | --- | --- | --- |
| Students for Fair Admissions, Inc. v. President & Fellows of Harvard College | 600 U.S. 181 | 2023 | Supreme Court of the United States | Race-based admissions programs at Harvard and UNC violate the Equal Protection Clause and Title VI; Grutter's permissive framework is effectively overruled. Race may be considered only as it relates to an applicant's individual experiences, never as a numerical 'plus' or category. |
| Parents Involved in Community Schools v. Seattle School District No. 1 | 551 U.S. 701 | 2007 | Supreme Court of the United States | K-12 race-based assignment plans intended to maintain racial balance fail strict scrutiny; achieving diversity for its own sake is not a compelling interest in K-12, and individualized consideration is required. |
| Grutter v. Bollinger | 539 U.S. 306 | 2003 | Supreme Court of the United States | Historical context (effectively overruled by SFFA): held that universities could consider race as one factor in admissions to achieve the educational benefits of diversity. |
| Adarand Constructors, Inc. v. Peña | 515 U.S. 200 | 1995 | Supreme Court of the United States | Race-based classifications by any level of government (federal or state) are subject to strict scrutiny; the same standard applies regardless of the racial group benefited or burdened. |

Canonical statutes: U.S. Const. amend. XIV, § 1 (Equal Protection Clause).

## Equal Protection levels of scrutiny

The tiered Equal Protection framework: strict scrutiny for suspect classifications and fundamental rights, intermediate scrutiny for sex and certain other classifications, and rational basis review for everything else.

Landmark cases:

| Case | Citation | Year | Court | Holding |
| --- | --- | --- | --- | --- |
| Craig v. Boren | 429 U.S. 190 | 1976 | Supreme Court of the United States | Established intermediate scrutiny for sex-based classifications: classifications must serve important governmental objectives and be substantially related to achievement of those objectives. |
| Loving v. Virginia | 388 U.S. 1 | 1967 | Supreme Court of the United States | Race-based classifications are subject to the most rigid scrutiny; struck down Virginia's anti-miscegenation statute as violative of both the Equal Protection and Due Process Clauses. |
| San Antonio Independent School District v. Rodriguez | 411 U.S. 1 | 1973 | Supreme Court of the United States | Wealth is not a suspect classification and education is not a fundamental right under the Federal Constitution; school funding through local property taxes survives rational-basis review. |
| Plyler v. Doe | 457 U.S. 202 | 1982 | Supreme Court of the United States | Texas could not deny free public education to children of undocumented immigrants; applied a heightened form of rational-basis review reflecting the unique status of children and the importance of education. |

Canonical statutes: U.S. Const. amend. XIV, § 1.

## First Amendment unprotected-speech categories

The categorical exceptions to First Amendment protection, including incitement (Brandenburg), fighting words (Chaplinsky), defamation of public figures (Sullivan), obscenity (Miller), and true threats.

Landmark cases:

| Case | Citation | Year | Court | Holding |
| --- | --- | --- | --- | --- |
| Brandenburg v. Ohio | 395 U.S. 444 | 1969 | Supreme Court of the United States | Speech advocating illegal conduct is unprotected only when directed to inciting or producing imminent lawless action and likely to produce such action; replaced the older 'clear and present danger' test. |
| Chaplinsky v. New Hampshire | 315 U.S. 568 | 1942 | Supreme Court of the United States | 'Fighting words', face-to-face epithets likely to provoke immediate violence, are not protected by the First Amendment; the category has been narrowed substantially in subsequent cases. |
| New York Times Co. v. Sullivan | 376 U.S. 254 | 1964 | Supreme Court of the United States | A public official may not recover for defamation about official conduct without proving actual malice: knowledge of falsity or reckless disregard for the truth. |
| Miller v. California | 413 U.S. 15 | 1973 | Supreme Court of the United States | Three-part test for obscenity: whether the average person, applying contemporary community standards, would find the work appeals to the prurient interest; whether it depicts sexual conduct in a patently offensive way as defined by state law; and whether, taken as a whole, it lacks serious literary, artistic, political, or scientific value. |

## Incitement (Brandenburg v. Ohio)

The First Amendment standard for punishing advocacy of force or law violation: directed to inciting imminent lawless action and likely to produce it.

Landmark cases:

| Case | Citation | Year | Court | Holding |
| --- | --- | --- | --- | --- |
| Brandenburg v. Ohio | 395 U.S. 444 | 1969 | Supreme Court of the United States | The government may not forbid advocacy of the use of force or of law violation except where such advocacy is directed to inciting or producing imminent lawless action and is likely to incite or produce such action. |

## Judicial review (Marbury v. Madison)

The power of the federal courts to review acts of Congress and the executive and to declare void those repugnant to the Constitution.

Landmark cases:

This established judicial review. |

## SEC v. Jarkesy - Seventh Amendment limits on agency adjudication

When the SEC seeks civil penalties for securities fraud, the Seventh Amendment guarantees a jury trial in an Article III court; the SEC may not adjudicate such claims through in-house ALJs.

Landmark cases:

| Case | Citation | Year | Court | Holding |
| --- | --- | --- | --- | --- |
| SEC v. Jarkesy | 603 U.S. 109 | 2024 | Supreme Court of the United States | When the SEC seeks civil penalties from a defendant for securities fraud, the Seventh Amendment requires a jury trial in an Article III court; the public-rights exception to Article III does not apply. |
| Axon Enterprise, Inc. v. FTC | 598 U.S. 175 | 2023 | Supreme Court of the United States | District courts have jurisdiction to hear structural constitutional challenges (e.g., to ALJ removal protections) to ongoing agency enforcement; plaintiffs need not wait for agency action to conclude before raising such challenges. |
| Free Enterprise Fund v. Public Company Accounting Oversight Board | 561 U.S. 477 | 2010 | Supreme Court of the United States | Dual for-cause removal protection for PCAOB members was unconstitutional because it impermissibly insulated executive officers from presidential oversight. |

## Second Amendment text-and-history test (Bruen)

The Second Amendment framework requiring government regulations to be consistent with the Nation's historical tradition of firearm regulation.

Landmark cases:

| Case | Citation | Year | Court | Holding |
| --- | --- | --- | --- | --- |
| New York State Rifle & Pistol Ass'n v. Bruen | 597 U.S. 1 | 2022 | Supreme Court of the United States | When the Second Amendment's plain text covers conduct, the government must demonstrate the regulation is consistent with the Nation's historical tradition of firearm regulation; means-end scrutiny is rejected. |
| District of Columbia v. Heller | 554 U.S. 570 | 2008 | Supreme Court of the United States | The Second Amendment protects an individual's right to keep and bear arms for self-defense, including in the home. |
| McDonald v. City of Chicago | 561 U.S. 742 | 2010 | Supreme Court of the United States | The Second Amendment is incorporated against the states through the Fourteenth Amendment's Due Process Clause. |
| United States v. Rahimi | 602 U.S. 680 | 2024 | Supreme Court of the United States | Refined the Bruen historical-tradition test: a regulation is consistent with the Second Amendment if a representative historical analogue exists; firearm prohibitions on those credibly threatening another's physical safety (18 U.S.C. § 922(g)(8)) satisfy that standard. |
| Caetano v. Massachusetts | 577 U.S. 411 | 2016 | Supreme Court of the United States | Per curiam reaffirmation that the Second Amendment extends to bearable arms not in existence at the founding (e.g., stun guns); Heller's analysis is not limited to weapons in common use at the time of ratification. |

Canonical statutes: U.S. Const. amend. II.

## Selective incorporation (Fourteenth Amendment)

The doctrine by which most Bill of Rights guarantees are applied to the states through the Fourteenth Amendment's Due Process Clause.

Landmark cases:

| --- | --- | --- | --- | --- |

## Thole v. U.S. Bank - Article III standing in defined-benefit ERISA plans

Participants in a fully funded defined-benefit ERISA plan lack Article III standing to sue under § 502(a)(2)/(3) for fiduciary breach where they have suffered no concrete injury to their guaranteed benefits.

Landmark cases:

| Case | Citation | Year | Court | Holding |
| --- | --- | --- | --- | --- |
| Thole v. U.S. Bank N.A. | 590 U.S. 538 | 2020 | Supreme Court of the United States | Participants in an over-funded defined-benefit plan lacked Article III standing to bring ERISA breach-of-fiduciary-duty claims because they had received all promised benefits and would receive the same benefits regardless of the lawsuit's outcome. |
