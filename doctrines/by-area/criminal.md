## Actual vs constructive possession in federal drug cases

The distinction between actual and constructive possession under federal drug-control law.

Landmark cases:

| Case | Citation | Year | Court | Holding |
| --- | --- | --- | --- | --- |
| United States v. Jones | 484 F.3d 783 | 2007 | Court of Appeals for the Fifth Circuit | Constructive possession requires knowledge of the contraband and dominion or control over it; mere proximity is insufficient. |
| United States v. Nungaray | 697 F.3d 1114 | 2012 | Court of Appeals for the Ninth Circuit | Constructive possession may be shown when a defendant has the power and intent to exercise dominion and control over a controlled substance, even when not in physical custody; knowledge plus a sufficient nexus to the drugs is required. |
| United States v. Hishaw | 235 F.3d 565 | 2000 | Court of Appeals for the Tenth Circuit | In jointly occupied premises, mere control over the location is insufficient to establish constructive possession of contraband; the government must show a particularized nexus between the defendant and the drugs. |

## Brady rule (prosecutorial disclosure)

The Due Process requirement that prosecutors disclose material evidence favorable to the accused.

Landmark cases:

| Case | Citation | Year | Court | Holding |
| --- | --- | --- | --- | --- |
| Brady v. Maryland | 373 U.S. 83 | 1963 | Supreme Court of the United States | Suppression by the prosecution of evidence favorable to the accused violates due process where the evidence is material to guilt or punishment, irrespective of good or bad faith. |
| Giglio v. United States | 405 U.S. 150 | 1972 | Supreme Court of the United States | Brady disclosure obligations include impeachment evidence affecting the credibility of prosecution witnesses, such as promises of leniency. |
| United States v. Bagley | 473 U.S. 667 | 1985 | Supreme Court of the United States | Materiality means a reasonable probability that, had the evidence been disclosed, the result of the proceeding would have been different. |
| Kyles v. Whitley | 514 U.S. 419 | 1995 | Supreme Court of the United States | The prosecution's Brady duty extends to evidence known only to police investigators; materiality is assessed cumulatively, not item-by-item. |

Canonical statutes: Fed. R. Crim. P. 16(a)(1).

## Burden of proof for affirmative defenses

A state may constitutionally require a defendant to prove an affirmative defense so long as the prosecution still proves every element of the offense beyond a reasonable doubt.

Landmark cases:

| Case | Citation | Year | Court | Holding |
| --- | --- | --- | --- | --- |
| Patterson v. New York | 432 U.S. 197 | 1977 | Supreme Court of the United States | A state may place on the defendant the burden of proving an affirmative defense (there, extreme emotional disturbance) by a preponderance of the evidence without violating due process, so long as it does not shift or relieve the prosecution's burden to prove each element of the crime beyond a reasonable doubt. |

## Civil RICO (18 USC 1962(c))

The elements of a civil claim under 18 U.S.C. § 1962(c) of the RICO statute.

Landmark cases:

| Case | Citation | Year | Court | Holding |
| --- | --- | --- | --- | --- |
| Sedima, S.P.R.L. v. Imrex Co. | 473 U.S. 479 | 1985 | Supreme Court of the United States | A § 1962(c) claim requires (1) conduct (2) of an enterprise (3) through a pattern (4) of racketeering activity. No prior criminal conviction is required. |
| H.J. Inc. v. Northwestern Bell Telephone Co. | 492 U.S. 229 | 1989 | Supreme Court of the United States | A 'pattern of racketeering' requires both relationship between predicate acts and continuity (or threat of continuity). |

## Eighth Amendment limit on felony-murder death penalty

Eighth Amendment limits on capital punishment for a felony-murder participant who did not kill, attempt to kill, or intend a killing.

Landmark cases:

| Case | Citation | Year | Court | Holding |
| --- | --- | --- | --- | --- |
| Enmund v. Florida | 458 U.S. 782 | 1982 | Supreme Court of the United States | The Eighth Amendment forbids the death penalty for a defendant who aids and abets a felony in which a murder is committed by others but who does not himself kill, attempt to kill, or intend that a killing take place or that lethal force be used. |
| Tison v. Arizona | 481 U.S. 137 | 1987 | Supreme Court of the United States | The death penalty may be imposed on a felony-murder participant who was a major participant in the underlying felony and acted with reckless indifference to human life, even absent an intent to kill. |

## Fourth Amendment exclusionary rule

The judge-made rule excluding evidence obtained in violation of the Fourth Amendment, and its exceptions (good faith, independent source, inevitable discovery, attenuation).

Landmark cases:

| Case | Citation | Year | Court | Holding |
| --- | --- | --- | --- | --- |
| Mapp v. Ohio | 367 U.S. 643 | 1961 | Supreme Court of the United States | Evidence obtained by an unconstitutional search and seizure is inadmissible in state criminal prosecutions; the exclusionary rule is incorporated against the states through the Fourteenth Amendment. |
| United States v. Leon | 468 U.S. 897 | 1984 | Supreme Court of the United States | Established the good-faith exception: evidence obtained by officers acting in objectively reasonable reliance on a facially valid warrant later found defective need not be suppressed. |
| Weeks v. United States | 232 U.S. 383 | 1914 | Supreme Court of the United States | Originated the federal exclusionary rule: evidence seized in violation of the Fourth Amendment may not be used by the federal government in a criminal prosecution. |
| Wong Sun v. United States | 371 U.S. 471 | 1963 | Supreme Court of the United States | Articulated the 'fruit of the poisonous tree' doctrine: evidence derivatively obtained from an unconstitutional search must be excluded unless the connection has become so attenuated as to dissipate the taint. |

Canonical statutes: Fed. R. Crim. P. 12(b)(3)(C).

## Fourth Amendment in digital surveillance - Carpenter / Smith / Riley

Carpenter v. United States held that long-term cell-site location records require a warrant; subsequent geofence-warrant cases (US v. Smith 5th Cir.; US v. Chatrie 4th Cir., 2024) divide on third-party-doctrine reach in the digital era.

Landmark cases:

| Case | Citation | Year | Court | Holding |
| --- | --- | --- | --- | --- |
| Carpenter v. United States | 585 U.S. 296 | 2018 | Supreme Court of the United States | The government's acquisition of seven days of historical cell-site location information (CSLI) constitutes a search under the Fourth Amendment and generally requires a warrant supported by probable cause. The third-party doctrine does not extend to the comprehensive chronicle of a person's movements revealed by CSLI. |
| Riley v. California | 573 U.S. 373 | 2014 | Supreme Court of the United States | Police generally may not, without a warrant, search digital information on a cell phone seized incident to arrest; the digital privacy stakes are too high to treat phones like cigarette packs. |
| Smith v. Maryland | 442 U.S. 735 | 1979 | Supreme Court of the United States | Historical context: established the third-party doctrine - there is no reasonable expectation of privacy in numbers dialed and conveyed to the phone company. Carpenter limits but does not overrule Smith. |
| United States v. Chatrie | 107 F.4th 319 | 2024 | Court of Appeals for the Fourth Circuit | Held that a Google geofence warrant for a 30-minute window in a bank-robbery investigation did not violate the Fourth Amendment because the defendant lacked a reasonable expectation of privacy in the Google Location History data revealed. |
| United States v. Smith | 110 F.4th 817 | 2024 | Court of Appeals for the Fifth Circuit | The Fifth Circuit reached the opposite conclusion from the Fourth Circuit in Chatrie: a geofence warrant directed at Google's Location History database is a general search that lacks particularity and probable cause, violating the Fourth Amendment. Defendants had a reasonable expectation of privacy in their LH data. |

Canonical statutes: U.S. Const. amend. IV; 18 U.S.C. § 2703(d) (Stored Communications Act).

## Fourth Amendment seizure (Terry / Mendenhall / Bostick)

When a person is 'seized' for Fourth Amendment purposes by police physical force or show of authority.

Landmark cases:

| Case | Citation | Year | Court | Holding |
| --- | --- | --- | --- | --- |
| Terry v. Ohio | 392 U.S. 1 | 1968 | Supreme Court of the United States | Permits brief investigatory stops based on reasonable suspicion; defines 'seizure' as occurring when an officer restrains a citizen's liberty. |
| United States v. Mendenhall | 446 U.S. 544 | 1980 | Supreme Court of the United States | A seizure occurs only when, in view of all the circumstances, a reasonable person would have believed she was not free to leave. |
| California v. Hodari D. | 499 U.S. 621 | 1991 | Supreme Court of the United States | A seizure by show of authority requires the suspect's submission; mere police pursuit of a fleeing suspect is not a seizure. |

Canonical statutes: U.S. Const. amend. IV.

A semiautomatic rifle equipped with a bump stock is not a 'machinegun' under 26 U.S.C. § 5845(b) because it does not fire more than one shot 'by a single function of the trigger'.

## Ineffective assistance of counsel (Strickland)

The Sixth Amendment two-part test for ineffective assistance of counsel.

Landmark cases:

| Case | Citation | Year | Court | Holding |
| --- | --- | --- | --- | --- |
| Strickland v. Washington | 466 U.S. 668 | 1984 | Supreme Court of the United States | Two-part test: counsel's performance was (1) deficient under an objective standard of reasonableness, and (2) prejudicial, a reasonable probability that, but for the error, the result would have been different. |
| United States v. Cronic | 466 U.S. 648 | 1984 | Supreme Court of the United States | Prejudice is presumed when there is a complete denial of counsel, when counsel fails to subject the prosecution's case to meaningful adversarial testing, or under similar extreme circumstances. |

## Miranda rights and custodial interrogation

The Fifth Amendment-derived requirement that police give specific warnings before custodial interrogation, and the framework for invocation, waiver, and exceptions.

Landmark cases:

| Case | Citation | Year | Court | Holding |
| --- | --- | --- | --- | --- |
| Miranda v. Arizona | 384 U.S. 436 | 1966 | Supreme Court of the United States | Before custodial interrogation, police must advise a suspect of the right to remain silent, that statements may be used against them, of the right to counsel, and the right to appointed counsel if indigent; statements obtained in violation are presumptively inadmissible. |
| Dickerson v. United States | 530 U.S. 428 | 2000 | Supreme Court of the United States | Miranda is a constitutional decision that may not be overturned by an Act of Congress; struck down 18 U.S.C. § 3501 as inconsistent with Miranda. |
| New York v. Quarles | 467 U.S. 649 | 1984 | Supreme Court of the United States | Recognized a public-safety exception to Miranda: police may ask questions reasonably prompted by a concern for public safety without first administering warnings, and the answers are admissible. |
| Berghuis v. Thompkins | 560 U.S. 370 | 2010 | Supreme Court of the United States | A suspect must invoke the right to remain silent unambiguously; mere silence during interrogation does not invoke the right, and a subsequent voluntary statement constitutes implied waiver. |

Canonical statutes: 18 U.S.C. § 3501.

## Sixth Amendment right to appointed counsel

The Sixth Amendment right to counsel is fundamental and obligatory on the states; indigent defendants charged with serious crimes are entitled to appointed counsel.

Landmark cases:

| Case | Citation | Year | Court | Holding |
| --- | --- | --- | --- | --- |
| Gideon v. Wainwright | 372 U.S. 335 | 1963 | Supreme Court of the United States | The Sixth Amendment's guarantee of counsel is a fundamental right made obligatory on the states by the Fourteenth Amendment; a state must provide counsel to an indigent defendant charged with a serious offense who cannot afford a lawyer. |
